01

Sender

Expand the sender details. Compare the full address and reply-to address with a known, legitimate contact. A display name can be forged.

02

Request

Ask why the sender needs this action now. Password resets, payroll changes and payment requests deserve independent verification.

Destination

Use the approved report-phishing button or security team process. Do not forward the message widely or test suspicious files yourself.

03

Common email lures

These patterns are useful clues, not proof on their own.

ACCOUNT ALERT

Password expiry

A message threatens immediate account closure and links to a sign-in page. Open the official app or type a saved, known address instead.

ATTACHMENT

Unexpected document

An invoice, shared file or delivery notice arrives without context. Confirm with the sender through a separate trusted channel before opening it.

MONEY REQUEST

Changed bank details

A supplier asks to update payment information. Verify changes using a previously known phone number and your organization’s approval procedure.

!

Remember: Good grammar, a familiar logo, a real colleague’s name or an encrypted connection does not prove that a message is safe. Verify the request and the route independently.

02

Read a URL from right to left

The registered domain is often the most important part to identify.

✓ STRUCTURE EXAMPLEhttps://login.example.com/account

Here, example.com is the domain; login is a subdomain.

! MISLEADING PATTERNhttps://example.com.attacker.invalid/login

The domain is attacker.invalid, not example.com. The trusted-looking words are only part of a longer hostname.

All addresses above are illustrative examples, not live destinations. Real URL parsing can include ports, encoded characters and redirects, so use your browser’s address bar and trusted bookmarks carefully.

03

Website trust checklist

Before signing in or paying, check more than the padlock.

01
Reach the site independently

Use a bookmark you saved earlier, the official app, or an address from trusted documentation—not a link from an unsolicited message.

02
Inspect the exact domain

Look for spelling swaps, extra words, unusual suffixes and domains that merely contain a brand name.

03
Question unusual data requests

A site asking for a password, one-time code, payment or identity documents unexpectedly should trigger a pause.

04
Do not trust design alone

Logos, professional layouts, HTTPS and padlock icons can appear on fraudulent sites too.

i

Safe browsing habit: If a page is asking for sensitive information, close it and navigate to the service using a trusted route. Never use a suspicious site to “test” whether your password works.

Understand the pressure tactics

Attackers often target emotions and habits rather than software flaws.

URGENCY

“Do it now”

SPECIAL TOPIC · SMS & VOICE

Scams can text.
Scams can call.

Smishing uses text messages. Vishing uses voice calls. Both may impersonate banks, delivery companies, government agencies, colleagues or technical support.

SMS warning signsCaller verification
TEXTS · CALLS · VOICE NOTES
✉

Smishing: suspicious text

Messages about parcel fees, account suspension, refunds or urgent verification may include shortened links and requests for payment.

  • Do not tap unexpected links.
  • Check delivery or bank status in the official app.
  • Do not reply with personal information.
☏

Vishing: suspicious call

A caller may create panic, claim to be from fraud prevention or ask you to move money to a “safe account”.

  • End the call if pressured.
  • Never share PINs, passwords or one-time codes.
  • Call the official number from your card or website.
◉

Caller ID can be spoofed

A familiar name or local number is not proof of identity. Scammers can manipulate caller ID and use convincing scripts.

  • Verify using a separate trusted channel.
  • Do not use a number supplied by the caller.
  • Ask a trusted person if unsure.

ACCOUNT SECURITY · LAYERED DEFENCE

Use unique passwords

Do not reuse the same password across services. A breach on one site should not unlock your other accounts.

  • Use official account security settings.

REFERENCE CENTRE

Glossary & frequently asked questions.

Quick definitions for common security terms, plus practical answers to questions learners often ask.

Phishing

A deceptive message designed to trick someone into revealing information, opening a harmful file or visiting a fraudulent site.

Smishing

Phishing delivered through SMS or other text messaging services.

Vishing

Social engineering carried out through voice calls or voice messages.

Quishing

Phishing that uses QR codes to direct people to malicious or deceptive destinations.

MFA

Multi-factor authentication: using more than one type of evidence to verify a sign-in.

Business Email Compromise

Fraud that impersonates a trusted person or compromises a business email account to influence payments or data sharing.

Spoofing

Falsifying an identity signal such as an email sender, caller ID or website appearance.

Credential theft

Stealing passwords, tokens or other sign-in information to gain access to accounts.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Use these rules as a starting point; always follow your organization's policies.

Does HTTPS mean a website is safe?

No. HTTPS encrypts the connection, but scammers can also use HTTPS. Verify the domain and the reason for the request.

What if I only opened a suspicious message?

Do not interact further. If you clicked a link, downloaded a file or entered information, report it and follow your organization's response process.

Should I forward a suspicious message to colleagues?

Usually no. Use your organization's reporting method so others are not exposed to the link or attachment.

Can a message from a known contact still be dangerous?

Yes. Accounts can be compromised and sender details can be spoofed. Verify unusual or sensitive requests separately.

Where should I report a suspected phishing attempt?

Use your workplace security team or the email, messaging or service provider's official reporting process. For financial fraud, contact your bank through a known official channel.

ADVANCED THREAT AWARENESS

AI & deepfake scams.

AI can make scam messages more fluent and voice or video impersonations more convincing. Do not rely on spelling mistakes or a familiar-sounding voice as your only test.

AI-assisted phishingVoice impersonationIndependent verification
VERIFY THE REQUEST, NOT THE APPEARANCE
01

Common AI-enabled fraud patterns

Technology changes the presentation; the safe response stays consistent.

A

Polished phishing messages

Messages may be grammatically perfect and tailored to your role. Judge the request, sender, context and destination—not writing quality alone.

B

Voice-cloned urgent calls

A caller may sound like a colleague or family member and demand money, gift cards or confidential information. Call back using a known number.

C

Fake video meetings

Video appearance is not sufficient proof for a high-risk request. Use a second channel and follow approval procedures for payments or access changes.

D

Personalized lures

Public posts, job titles and company information can be used to make a message sound credible. Unexpected requests still need verification.

02

High-assurance verification protocol

Use these controls whenever money, credentials, identity documents or confidential data are involved.

  1. Pause the transaction. Do not transfer funds, reveal a code or change account details while under pressure.
  2. Switch channels. Contact the person through a number or directory entry you already trust—not details supplied in the request.
  3. Use a challenge phrase or callback process. For teams that handle high-risk approvals, agree on a documented verification procedure in advance.
  4. Require dual approval. Follow finance or identity-management rules; a familiar voice must not bypass normal controls.
  5. Report the attempt. Share the time, channel and requested action with your security team, without spreading the suspicious link.

Common identity attack patterns

Recognize the risk and choose the appropriate defensive action.

Password reuse
